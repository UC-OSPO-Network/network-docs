Open Science

2000 - 2003 · UC Santa Cruz, UC San Diego

The early 2000s produced three projects that redefined what open science software could be. None of them was trying to build a platform. One was racing a supercomputer. Two were trying to make sense of data that no existing tool could handle. All three became the field standard.

The UCSC Genome Browser: Three Days Before a Supercomputer

The project: UCSC Genome Browser Campus: UC Santa Cruz Period: Summer 2000 Key figures: Jim Kent (PhD student), David Haussler (faculty)

Section in preparation.

What it became

The UCSC Genome Browser (Kent et al., 2002) is the reference tool for genomic research worldwide. It hosts assemblies for hundreds of species, is updated continuously, and remains the first stop for any researcher navigating genomic data.

EEGLAB: The Most-Cited Scientific Software You’ve Never Heard Of

The project: EEGLAB Campus: UC San Diego (Swartz Center for Computational Neuroscience) Period: 1997 - 2001 (first developed at the Salk Institute; later at UC San Diego) Key figures: Scott Makeig

Section in preparation.

What it became

EEGLAB is an open source environment for processing EEG and related electrophysiological signals. The 2004 paper that introduced it (Delorme & Makeig, 2004) had about 26,800 citations in OpenAlex in November 2026 (OpenAlex, 2026).

Cytoscape: Mapping the Cell

The project: Cytoscape Campus: UC San Diego, with the Institute for Systems Biology (Seattle) and the Whitehead Institute Period: 2003 Key figures: Trey Ideker and collaborators

Section in preparation.

What it became

Cytoscape (Shannon et al., 2003) is a widely used platform for network biology. Researchers use it to visualize disease pathways, protein interactions, and molecular networks. Its developers reported over 300,000 downloads a year in 2025 (Ono et al., 2025).
